The Fourier Transform

How mathematics hears which notes are hidden in a chord.

15 May 2026

An instrument plays three notes at once, but you hear a single chord. Physically, all three notes create pressure waves that sum together and hit your eardrum as one. Your brain is somehow able to recognize the three distinct frequencies hidden in that single wave. The mechanism behind how the brain does this is an extremely complicated topic, but getting machines to decompose the signal is quite straightforward, and this post explains how it is done. The core mathematical operation doing signal decomposition is called the Fourier Transform.

Sound is a sum of sinusoids

A pure tone (a tuning fork or a whistle) is a sinusoid. A sinusoid is a smooth, repetitive oscillation at a single frequency. If you stack multiple pure tones on top of each other, you get a chord. The sounds you hear in your ear on a daily basis are typically a sum of hundreds of pure tones. When looking at the waveforms of chords, it looks like pure noise (see Figure 1), but it isn’t noise. The pure tones are just hiding in the sum of frequencies of the chord signal.

Below you can hear three different pure tones and the chord they produce when combined. Then below the media players, you can see the wave forms of the signals individually and then the signal that is produced when combining the three pure tones.

fs = 8000
t = np.linspace(0, 1.5, int(fs * 1.5), endpoint=False)
note_freqs = [300, 700, 1200]  # Hz ratio 3:7:12, matching the figure below
note_amps = [1.0, 0.7, 0.4]
chord = sum(a * np.cos(2 * np.pi * f * t) for f, a in zip(note_freqs, note_amps))

How to detect a hidden frequency

The core intuition behind finding the frequencies in a chord is that if the frequency fff is present in the signal, then the signal and a pure tone at fff should “agree” on average. “Agreeing” in this context means that when the test pure tone is positive, the signal tends to be positive, and when the test tone is negative, the signal also tends to be negative.

The mathematical way to measure this agreement is the dot product. In practice, you multiply the signal and the test sinusoid at each discrete time point and then sum all the multiplications together (aka you take the dot product). If the products are consistently positive (or consistently negative) the sum is large and this means that the test frequency can be found from the original signal. If the test frequency doesn’t match with the original signal, the products alternate in sign and cancel out and the sum collapses to zero. This is the entire intuition of the fourier transform. The fourier transform just tests every single frequency and then returns a list of frequencies present.

Geometric intuition: winding a signal around a circle

There is a nice geometric way to understand what Equation 1 is doing. The idea is that you take the signal and wind it around a circle. At sample nnn, place the value x[n]x[n]x[n] on the complex plane, rotated by kkk full turns spread over NNN samples. Then each point becomes a point in 2D space. The sum X[k]X[k]X[k] is the centre of mass of all these points.

When kkk matches a hidden frequency, the wound samples bunch up on one side of the origin (large ∣X[k]∣|X[k]|∣X[k]∣). When kkk does not match a hidden frequency, the samples scatter evenly around the circle (small ∣X[k]∣|X[k]|∣X[k]∣). From the figure below, you can see the chord signal that is wound around a circle. Changing the frequency kkk shows on which frequencies ∣X[k]∣|X[k]|∣X[k]∣ becomes non-zero.
