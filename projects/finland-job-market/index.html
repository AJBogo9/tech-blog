The Finnish Job Market in 2026

Charts and an explorer over public data on the Finnish labour market. The data covers the EU comparison, the Labour Force Survey, and the employment services' jobseekers and vacancies by region and occupation.

Explore the data

Pick a data set and a measure, then tick the lines to compare in the list next to the chart, or under it on a phone. The list shows each option’s latest value, so you can sort it to find the highest. The 12-month average removes the seasonal swing in the employment service data, and the index puts lines of different size on one scale. The CSV link under the chart downloads exactly what it shows. The page address keeps the view, so a link to it opens the same chart.

About the data

The charts come from three sources. Eurostat and Statistics Finland both publish the Labour Force Survey. The KEHA Centre, a state agency, counts the people registered with the public employment services. The table compares their numbers for September 2026.

Some registered jobseekers do not meet the survey’s definition of unemployed, and some of the people the survey counts as unemployed are not registered. In recent years the survey’s yearly average has been 50,000 to 135,000 lower than the register’s. In some spring months the survey’s count has been the higher one, because students looking for work count in the survey and not in the register [6].

Eurostat: the EU comparison

Eurostat publishes a monthly unemployment rate for each country from that country’s labour force survey. A person counts as unemployed if they are 15 to 74, had no work in the survey week, looked for work in the four weeks before and could start within two weeks. The definition is the International Labour Organization’s and is the same in every country [7], except that Spain and Italy count people from age 16 [8].

Eurostat’s rate for Finland differs a little from Statistics Finland’s own seasonally adjusted rate, because Eurostat leaves out conscripts [9] and puts its rate together from separately adjusted parts [10]. Eurostat revises recent months as new data arrives. Spain’s latest months are forecasts, and Estonia’s figures are three-month moving averages [7].

Statistics Finland: the Labour Force Survey

The survey interviews about 12,500 people a month, so every figure has a margin of error. For September 2026 it estimated 298,000 unemployed before seasonal adjustment, ±24,000 at 95 % confidence [11, 9].

Figure 2 uses the trend series, which removes both the seasonal pattern and the month-to-month noise. Its last few months are partly estimated by a statistical model and can change when new months come in [2].

KEHA Centre: the employment service statistics

These numbers are counts from the register of the public employment services, taken on the last weekday of each month. A registered jobseeker counts as unemployed if they have no job, do not work full time as an entrepreneur and are not a full-time student [12]. The unemployment rate divides the count by a workforce figure from Statistics Finland’s register-based employment statistics, usually about two years old [3].

Open vacancies are only the jobs that employers reported to the employment services [12, 13]. In 2015, 53 % of workplaces with open vacancies had used the employment services [14].

In 2024 and 2025 the employment services ran an old and a new client system side by side, and the statistics sometimes showed too few open vacancies, for example in June 2024. The new system replaced the old one in May 2025 [13]. For parts of that period Figure 3 may show too many jobseekers per vacancy.

The regional table lists vacancies with a flexible or unknown location as regions of their own, 8,300 of the 26,758 in September 2026 [3]. The explorer leaves them out, so each region shows more jobseekers per vacancy than it would with those jobs counted.

Jobseekers are classified by the occupation recorded for them and vacancies by the occupation of the job [15]. The classification is based on Statistics Finland’s classification of occupations [16], and its four-digit codes follow the international ISCO-08. The documentation does not say whether a jobseeker’s occupation is the job they last held or the one they are looking for, so people in machine learning, data and software jobs may also be counted under other codes.

The occupation table hides some counts for confidentiality [5], so small occupations have months without a value, most often in the vacancy counts.

At the start of 2025 the employment services moved from the state’s TE Offices to employment areas run by the municipalities. The government’s announcement of the reform says it alters the recorded data considerably [17], so comparisons across 2025 need care.
